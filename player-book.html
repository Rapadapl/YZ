    Root & Claw · Core rules

    Player's book

    Everything the person holding a character sheet needs: how a roll works, how a hero is built, and how they grow after the first session.

    The classic variant
    A pool of d6. Attributes run from 1 to 5, skills from 0 to 5. Add them together and roll that many dice; every 6 is a success.
    The modern variant
    Two ranked dice per check. Attributes and skills run from A to D, where A is d12 and D is d6. Any 6 or higher succeeds.
    01

    How a roll works

    A skill check answers two questions at once: did the action succeed, and what did it cost. The dice measure the outcome of one specific attempt under pressure, not how good a hero is in general.

    The classic pool

    Take dice equal to the character's skill level plus the linked attribute, plus any bonus dice from gear, and roll them together. Each 6 is a success, and one success is enough. Without the skill, roll the attribute dice alone.

    The modern pair

    Roll one die for the skill rank and one for the attribute rank. A 6 or higher on either die is a success, and a 10 or higher on a single die counts as two — possible only on a d10 or d12. Without the skill, roll the attribute die alone.

    Success and effect

    One success finishes the job. Every success beyond the first buys an extra effect, agreed with the gamemaster: the hero acts faster, stays quiet, helps a companion, impresses someone, or notices something nobody else did. In combat each extra success adds 1 point of damage.

    No successes

    A roll with no successes is a failure, and something goes wrong. Describe what went wrong and how the story moves on — the action may still happen, but at a price in time, noise, equipment or position. Failure must never stop the game: there is always a way forward.

    Pushing the roll

    A character can lean into an action and push the roll: reroll every die that did not come up 1. Dice showing 1 are banes and stay where they are. After a push the new result stands, including dice that were not rerolled.

    A roll can only be pushed once, and only when the character is actively acting: passive rolls, such as noticing an ambush, cannot be pushed. What the push costs — wounds, psychosis, conditions, attribute points or the complications the world answers with — is covered in chapter 7.

    02

    Creating a character

    Character creation is seven steps, in order. Each step narrows the next: the archetype suggests who the hero was, the numbers say what they can do now, and the personality traits say what they will risk everything for.

    Step 1 — Choose an archetype

    An archetype is a role, profession or past: enforcer, scavenger, healer, archivist, hunter. It fixes what the character was, suggests attributes, skills, specialty and starting gear, and gives the table a shared picture within a minute. It is a starting point, not a cage.

    Step 2 — Determine attributes

    Four attributes describe the body and the mind: Strength is raw muscle and brawn; Agility is body control, speed and motor skills; Wits is perception, intelligence and sanity; Empathy is charisma and the ability to read and move people.

    Classic Distribute 14 points across the four attributes. No attribute gets less than 2 or more than 5. Each archetype has a key attribute that may reach 5; every other attribute is limited to 4.

    Wounds

    Wounds are health. A hero has 10 boxes; a beast or NPC uses the HP value on its card. When the boxes are full, the character is taken out and takes a critical injury. There is no separate resolve: psychosis handles the mind (chapter 05).

    Classic

    • Wounds: 10 boxes for a hero and an ally; a beast uses the HP on its card.

    Modern

    • Wounds: the same 10 boxes — the variant does not change the health pool.

    Step 3 — Determine skills

    The twelve basic skills are covered in chapter 3. Skills are what the character has actually practised, and they are always read together with an attribute.

    Classic Distribute 10 points across the starting skills. A skill from the archetype's list can start at level 3; every other skill is limited to level 1. A skill at 0 is not a prohibition: the hero can still try, rolling attribute dice alone.
    Modern Choose one skill at rank B, two at rank C and three at rank D; the rest stay unranked. The B must normally be one of the archetype's skills; the rest are free. Unranked skills still roll on the attribute die alone.

    2—Trained

    Step 4 — Pick a specialty

    A specialty is a technique, a trick or a narrow field of expertise, narrower than a skill and giving a small, reliable edge inside it. A character usually starts with one, limited by the archetype, and learns more in play — always from a teacher who already has it.

    Step 5 — Personality traits

    Six short statements turn a set of numbers into a person, described in chapter 4. They are also the main engine of experience: playing to them is how a hero advances.

    Step 6 — Take starting gear

    Write down everything the hero carries, one item per line. Clothes and the container itself do not count against the load limit; the contents do. Starting gear usually follows from the archetype.

    Step 7 — Name the character

    A name, and perhaps one sentence about where it came from. That is enough to begin.

    Worked example, classic. An enforcer takes Strength 5, Agility 4, Wits 3, Empathy 2 — 14 points, key attribute at 5. Skills: Force 3, Melee 3, Marksmanship 1, the rest at 0. Her specialty is grappling. Her pride is “never backs down from a challenge”, and her weakness is a debt she cannot repay. Wounds are the same 10 boxes every hero has; beasts use their own HP value.

    Worked example, modern. The same enforcer raises Strength to A and Agility to B, and lowers Empathy from C to D to buy a third and fourth increase, ending at Strength A, Agility B, Wits C, Empathy D. One skill at B (Melee), two at C (Force, Stamina), three at D (Marksmanship, Observation, Persuasion). Wounds stay 10 boxes: the variant does not change the health pool.

    03

    The twelve skills

    Every skill belongs to one attribute. The pairing is fixed: it tells you which die or how many dice to add, and it explains why a strong character is not automatically good at everything physical.

    Basic skills and their attributes
    SkillAttributeWhat it covers
    ForceStrengthLifting, pushing or breaking heavy and stubborn things
    MeleeStrengthAttacking and defending with melee weapons or bare hands
    StaminaStrengthEndurance: holding your breath, resisting poison, staying alive when critically injured
    MarksmanshipAgilityFiring ranged weapons
    MobilityAgilityRisky movement and precise work: running, jumping, climbing, retreating
    StealthAgilityStaying unseen and unheard, and picking pockets
    CraftingWitsRepairing what is broken, building what is missing
    ObservationWitsReading a place, searching it, noticing what is out of place
    SurvivalWitsWeather, terrain, hunger, thirst and the things that hunt you
    HealingEmpathyStopping blood, setting bones, keeping someone alive
    InsightEmpathyReading people and catching lies
    PersuasionEmpathyConvincing, calming, bargaining, lying well

    Some games add skills of their own, though more than sixteen starts to spread a character thin.

    Specializations

    A specialization never replaces the skill — it adds to it inside a narrow situation. Most are simply on or off; some are tiered, usually with three ranks, each unlocking another effect. A character learns a specialization only from someone who already has it, which makes teachers a resource worth protecting.

    Sample specializations
    SpecializationEffect
    BodyguardDive in front of an attack on someone within Short range; roll Mobility, not an action.

    04

    Personality traits

    Personality traits are the part of the character sheet that pushes the story. Each one is a sentence, and several of them earn experience when they make the hero's life harder.

    The six traits
    TraitWhat it isAt the table
    PrideWhat the hero believes about themselves and will not let go ofOnce per session, one automatic success on a roll — if you can justify how pride helps
    WeaknessThe crack in the armour: a fear, a habit, a hungerPlaying to it earns extra experience at the end of the session
    Dark secretSomething that happened before the game beganA hook for the gamemaster; it can earn extra experience after a session
    Big dreamThe long goal the hero would sacrifice forExtra experience for risking or sacrificing something significant to reach it
    RelationshipsOne sentence per other hero: how you stand with themGives the gamemaster handles to pull, and you reasons to act
    BuddyThe one character you are closest toExtra experience for making a sacrifice or taking a big risk for them

    After any session you may change your traits — pride, weakness, dark secret, big dream, relationships — ideally connecting the change to something that happened in play.

    Why they matter

    A character who only shoots straight earns experience slowly. A character who cannot walk away from a fight, who owes the wrong people, or who goes back for a friend, earns it every session — and gives the table something to play against.

    05

    Equipment and load

    Carrying capacity is measured in regular items: a knife, a rope, a day of food, a box of matches. What you carry is what you have when the plan falls apart.

    Classic

    The carry limit equals twice the Strength score. Strength 4 carries eight regular items.

    Modern

    The carry limit equals the die size of the Strength rank. Strength A carries twelve items, Strength D six.

    Heavy, light and tiny

    Heavy items count as two, three or more regular items — a rifle, a jerrycan, a stretcher. Light items count as ½ or even ¼ — a lens, a lockpick set, a letter. Tiny items fit in a closed fist and do not count at all, but they still get written down. Forgotten inventory is lost inventory.

    Backpacks, mounts and vehicles

    A backpack carries an additional number of items equal to the carry limit, but all Mobility rolls take −2 while it is worn. A mount carries its own carry limit, and twice that if you dismount and lead it; vehicles carry more still.

    Consumables

    Food, water, ammunition, torches and power can be tracked two ways. Count them one by one — a ration, ten arrows or 25 bullets count as ¼ of a regular item — or give each type a supply rating. Each supply counts as one regular item, whatever its rating. When the gamemaster calls for a supply roll, roll a number of d6 equal to the rating, never more than six; every 1 reduces the rating by one, and at zero the resource is gone. Giving a consumable away simply moves rating steps from one character to another.

    06

    Modifiers, help and opposition

    Most checks need no modification. When the situation clearly favours or hinders the hero, the gamemaster applies one modifier, and the table moves on.

    Where the dice come from

    When dice are lost, take them in this order: skill dice first, then gear, then attribute dice. If nothing is left to roll, the attempt is impossible as things stand — a new approach is needed, not a new roll.

    Modern A modifier steps a base die up or down rather than adding dice. Step up the lower die first and downstep the higher one; never go above two d12s, never below one d6. An advantage adds a third die of the same type as the lower one, a disadvantage removes the lower die, and the two cancel each other one for one — only one of either ever applies.

    Help

    Another character can help if they are present and able to affect the outcome, declared before the dice are rolled. Each helper adds +1, to a maximum of +3. In combat, helping costs an action of the same kind as the action being helped.

    Opposed checks

    Both sides roll and compare successes; every success on one side cancels one on the other. Whoever has successes left wins. Only the active party may push, and may decide to do so after seeing the opponent's roll. Equal totals are a tie: the active party typically fails, and if the table needs a decision, reroll the contest.

    Passive checks

    Noticing an ambush, resisting a lie, staying calm — these are not actions, so they cannot be pushed (chapter 7). Roll them plainly and take the result. NPCs make the same rolls, and the gamemaster only rolls when their action affects a player character.

    07

    Special effort

    When everything is on the line, a hero can dig deeper: reroll every die that did not come up 1. The 1s stay where they are — they are banes, and they are what the effort costs.

    Once per check

    A roll can be pushed once, and only when the character is actively acting: passive rolls cannot be pushed. A spell's casting roll cannot be pushed either. After the push the new result stands, including dice that were not rerolled.

    What the price looks like

    The system offers several ways to charge for the effort; a game uses one of them, chosen before play begins.

    Cost of pushing, by variant
    CostHow it works
    Wounds and psychosisOne wound per bane if the roll used Body or Agility; one psychosis per bane if it used Wits or Empathy.
    Attribute damageOne point of damage per bane on the attribute dice rolled, and one step of gear bonus lost per bane on gear dice. Damage that breaks you this way never causes a critical injury.
    Wounds for everyoneA hero and an ally have 10 wound boxes; a beast uses the HP value on its card. Wounds are health, not a separate pool.
    ConditionsOne condition of your choice per push — physical for Strength and Agility, mental for Wits and Empathy.
    The world answersFor every push the gamemaster answers with a complication: a weapon misfires, something is dropped, reinforcements arrive. No separate currency, and its size follows the number of banes.

    A scavenger with Strength 3 and Force 3 rolls six dice to force a hatch, and comes up with two 1s and no sixes. She pushes: four dice are rerolled, two successes come up — and she takes two points of damage, or two conditions, depending on the game's chosen cost.

    08

    Experience and advancement

    Experience is awarded at the end of a session, by the group, in answer to six questions. Each honest yes is a point; the gamemaster has the final word.

    • Did the character take part in the session?
    • Did the character explore a new location?
    • Did the character defeat one or more dangerous adversaries?
    • Did the character overcome an obstacle without using force?
    • Did the character act on their weakness, dark secret, big dream or relationships?
    • Did the character do something else extraordinary?

    Points are spent during a rest, or between sessions. A skill rises one step at a time, and only if it was used successfully since the last advance — or if a teacher with a higher skill trained the character for a shift. Learning a new skill at level 1, or rank D, costs 5 XP.

    Specializations always cost 10 XP. They cannot be learned alone: the hero needs a teacher who already has it, teaching for at least one shift, and a successful Persuasion roll by the teacher at the end of that shift. Failure costs nothing but the shift — the experience is kept, and the teacher can try again.

    Advancement in one line

    Use the skill to earn the right to raise it, find someone who knows more than you to learn something new, and pay for both in experience.

    First session checklist

    • Archetype chosen and named
    • Four attributes written down, key attribute marked
    • Twelve skills with levels or ranks
    • One specialty
    • Pride, weakness, dark secret, big dream, relationships and a buddy
    • Equipment listed, load added up
    • Wounds written down: 10 boxes for a hero, the HP value for a beast
    The whole game in three sentences

    Say what you do and how. Roll the dice the character has earned. Live with the result, and let it change the situation.
