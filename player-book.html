    Year Zero Engine · System reference

    Player's book

    Everything the person holding a character sheet needs: how a roll works, how a hero is built, and how they grow after the first session.

    The classic variant
    A pool of d6. Characteristics run from 1 to 5, skills from 0 to 5. Add them together and roll that many dice; every 6 is a success.
    The modern variant
    Two ranked dice per check. Characteristics and skills run from A to D, where A is d12 and D is d6. Any 6 or higher succeeds.
    01

    How a roll works

    A skill check answers two questions at once: did the action succeed, and what did it cost. The dice do not measure how well a hero performs in general — they measure the outcome of one specific attempt under pressure.

    The classic pool

    Take dice equal to the character's skill level plus the linked characteristic. Roll them all at once. Each die showing a 6 is one success; a single success is enough to succeed. If the hero lacks the skill, roll only the characteristic dice, and add extra dice for helpful equipment.

    The modern pair

    Roll one die for the skill rank and one for the characteristic rank. A result of 6 or higher on either die is a success. When a die shows 10 or more — possible only on the d10 and d12 — it counts as two successes.

    Success and effect

    One success finishes the job. Every success beyond the first buys an extra effect, agreed with the gamemaster: the hero acts faster, stays quiet, helps a companion, or notices something nobody else did. In combat each extra success adds 1 point of damage.

    No successes

    A roll with no successes is a failure with a consequence. Describe what went wrong and how the story moves on — the action may still happen, but at a price in time, noise, equipment or position. Failure should never stop the game.

    A character can also push a roll further, at a cost. That is a special effort, covered in chapter 7.

    02

    Creating a character

    Character creation is seven steps, in order. Each step narrows the next: the archetype suggests who the hero was, the numbers say what they can do now, and the personality edges say what they will risk everything for.

    Step 1 — Choose an archetype

    An archetype is a role, profession or past: enforcer, scavenger, healer, archivist, hunter. It fixes the starting package of characteristics, skills and equipment, and gives everyone at the table a shared picture of the character within a minute. It is a starting point, not a cage.

    Step 2 — Determine characteristics

    Four characteristics describe the body and the mind. Body is raw strength and endurance; Dexterity is coordination and speed; Wits is perception, intelligence and common sense; Empathy is charisma and the ability to read and move people.

    Classic Distribute 14 points across the four characteristics. No characteristic starts below 2 or above 5, and the archetype's key characteristic is the only one that may reach 5 — the others cap at 4.
    Modern All four characteristics begin at rank C (d8). You have three steps to raise ranks during creation. You may lower one characteristic from C to D to gain a fourth step. No characteristic may end above A.

    Step 3 — Determine skills

    The twelve basic skills are covered in chapter 3. Skills are what the character has actually practised, and they are always read together with a characteristic.

    Classic Skills run from 0 to 5. Spend your starting levels on the skills the archetype implies. A skill at 0 is not a prohibition: the hero can still try, rolling characteristic dice alone.
    Modern Skills run from A to D. Choose one skill at rank B, two at rank C and three at rank D; the rest stay unranked. The B usually goes to an archetype skill.

    Step 4 — Pick a specialization

    A specialization is a technique, a trick or a narrow field of expertise: lockpicking rather than general Craft, knife fighting rather than all close combat. It is narrower than a skill and gives a small, reliable edge inside that narrow field. Most characters start with one; more are earned in play.

    Step 5 — Choose personality edges

    Six short statements turn a set of numbers into a person. They are described in chapter 4, and they are the main engine of experience: playing to them is how a hero advances.

    Step 6 — Take starting equipment

    Write down everything the hero carries. Clothes and the container itself do not count against the load limit; the contents do. Starting gear usually follows from the archetype.

    Step 7 — Name the character

    A name, and perhaps one sentence about where it came from. That is enough to begin.

    Worked example, classic. An enforcer takes Body 5, Dexterity 4, Wits 3, Empathy 2 — 14 points, key characteristic at 5. Skills: Skelm 3, Near Combat 3, Ranged 1. Her specialization is grappling. Her Pride is “never backs down from a challenge”, and her Weakness is a debt she cannot repay. She starts with a crowbar, a revolver and a backpack.

    Worked example, modern. The same enforcer raises Body to A and Dexterity to B, and lowers Empathy from C to D to buy a third and fourth step, ending at Body A, Dexterity B, Wits C, Empathy D. One skill at B (Near Combat), two at C (Skelm, Endurance), three at D (Ranged, Observation, Persuasion).

    03

    The twelve skills

    Every skill belongs to one characteristic. The pairing is fixed: it tells you which die or how many dice to add, and it explains why a strong character is not automatically good at everything physical.

    EnduranceBodyStamina: holding your breath, resisting poison, marching on

    Specializations

    A specialization never replaces the skill — it adds to it inside a narrow situation. Some carry ranks, usually three, each unlocking another effect; others are simply on or off. A character can learn a specialization only from someone who already has it, which makes teachers a resource worth protecting.

    04

    Personality edges

    Personality edges are the part of the character sheet that pushes the story. Each one is a sentence, and each one can earn experience when it makes the hero's life harder.

    The six edges
    EdgeWhat it isAt the table
    PrideWhat the hero believes about themselves and will not let go ofOnce per game session, gain one automatic success on a check — if you can explain how pride helps
    WeaknessThe crack in the armour: a fear, a habit, a hungerWhen the weakness causes real trouble, it earns extra experience at the end of the session
    Dark secretSomething that happened before the game beganA hook for the gamemaster; revealing or protecting it changes what experience you earn
    DreamThe long goal the hero would sacrifice forExperience for risking something real in its pursuit
    RelationshipsOne phrase per other hero: how you stand with themGives the gamemaster handles to pull, and you reasons to act
    Best friendThe one character you would go back forExperience for risking or sacrificing something significant for them
    Why they matter

    A character who only shoots straight earns experience slowly. A character who cannot walk away from a fight, who owes the wrong people, or who goes back for a friend, earns it every session — and gives the table something to play against.

    05

    Equipment and load

    Carrying capacity is measured in ordinary items: a knife, a rope, a day of food, a box of matches. What you carry is what you have when the plan falls apart.

    Classic Comfortable load equals twice the Body value. A hero with Body 4 carries eight ordinary items.
    Modern Comfortable load equals the number of faces on the Body rank's die: Body A carries twelve items, Body D carries six.

    Heavy, light and small

    • Heavy items count as two, three or more ordinary items — a rifle, a jerrycan, a stretcher.
    • Light items count as a half or a quarter — a lens, a lockpick set, a letter.
    • Small items fit in a fist and can be hidden, but still get written down. Forgotten inventory is lost inventory.

    Backpacks, animals and vehicles

    A worn backpack doubles the load a hero can carry, at the cost of a −2 modifier to Dexterity checks. Pack animals and vehicles carry their own maximum load, and a led animal rather than a ridden one carries double.

    Supplies instead of counting

    Food, water, fuel, ammunition, torches and batteries can be tracked two ways. Count them one by one, which is exact and tedious, or give each type a supply rating. Each supply occupies one ordinary item, and whenever the gamemaster calls for a consumption check, roll dice equal to the current rating (maximum six). Each success reduces the rating by one. At zero, that resource is gone.

    06

    Modifiers, help and opposition

    Most checks need no modification. When the situation clearly favours or hinders the hero, the gamemaster applies one modifier, and the table moves on.

    Difficulty in the classic variant
    DifficultyModifier
    Elementary+3 dice
    Simple+2 dice
    Easy+1 die
    Standard0
    Not easy−1 die
    Hard−2 dice
    Extreme−3 dice

    When a check must lose dice, take them in this order: skill dice first, then equipment, then characteristic dice. If nothing is left to roll, the attempt is impossible as things stand — a new approach is needed, not a new roll.

    Modern Advantages and penalties work differently. An advantage adds a third die, equal to the smaller of the skill and characteristic ranks. A penalty removes the smaller of the two dice. An advantage and a penalty cancel each other, and only one modifier can remain after cancellation.

    Help

    Another character can help if they are close enough and able to affect the outcome, declared before the dice are rolled. Each helper adds +1, to a maximum of +3. In combat, helping costs the same action the helped character is spending.

    Opposed checks

    Both sides roll and compare successes; every success on one side cancels one on the other. Whoever has successes left wins. Only the character who initiated the contest may make a special effort, and only after seeing the opponent's roll. Equal totals are a tie: usually the instigator loses, and if the table needs a decision, roll the contest again.

    Passive checks

    Noticing an ambush, resisting a lie, staying calm — these are not actions, so they cannot be pushed with a special effort (chapter 7). Roll them plainly and take the result.

    07

    Special effort

    When everything is on the line, a hero can dig deeper: reroll every die that did not come up 1. The 1s stay where they are — they are curses, and they are what the effort costs.

    Once per check

    A special effort can be made once on any single check, and only when the character is actively acting. It cannot be used on a passive check.

    What the price looks like

    The system offers several ways to charge for the effort; a game uses one of them, chosen before play begins.

    Cost of a special effort, by variant
    CostHow it works
    Wounds and stressOne point of damage per curse if the skill is tied to Body or Dexterity; one point of stress per curse if it is tied to Wits or Empathy.
    Characteristic reductionEach curse immediately lowers the characteristic used, until the scene ends. Skill and equipment dice are not affected.
    Stress diceEach curse adds a stress die (d6) to later checks. Stress dice can produce successes on a 6, but a 1 on a stress die forces a panic check.
    ConditionsEach curse gives a condition — exhausted, annoyed, frightened — with a penalty attached to particular checks.
    Fate pointsThe gamemaster gains a fate point for every effort made, to spend later on obstacles and bad luck.

    A scavenger with Body 3 and Skelm 3 rolls six dice to force a hatch, and comes up with two 1s and no sixes. She pushes: four dice are rerolled, two successes come up — and she takes two points of damage, or two conditions, depending on the game's chosen cost.

    08

    Experience and advancement

    Experience is awarded at the end of a session, by the group, in answer to six questions. Each honest yes is a point.

    1. Did the character take part in the session?
    2. Did the character explore somewhere new?
    3. Did the character defeat a dangerous opponent?
    4. Did the character overcome an obstacle without violence?
    5. Did the character act on a personality edge?
    6. Did the character do something outstanding?

    Points are spent between sessions, or during a rest. A skill rises by one step at a time, and only if it was used successfully since the last advance — practice is the permission slip.

    Specializations always cost 10 experience. They cannot be learned alone: the hero needs a teacher who already has it, a full shift of training for both of them, and a successful Persuasion check by the teacher at the end of that shift.

    Advancement in one line

    Use the skill to earn the right to raise it, find someone who knows more than you to learn something new, and pay for both in experience.

    • Twelve skills with levels or ranks

    Say what you do and how. Roll the dice the character has earned. Live with the result, and let it change the situation.
