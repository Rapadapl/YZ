    Root & Claw · Core rules

    Master's book

    The rules the gamemaster runs: how violence resolves, what it costs, how magic bends the world, and what the road takes from people who travel it.

    The classic variant
    Pools of d6. Damage is a number of points, armour is a number of dice, and wounds and stress are counted separately.
    The modern variant
    Ranked dice from A to D. Damage and armour talk in ranks and reliability, and the same wound can also be paid as a condition.
    01

    Running the game

    Roll when the outcome is uncertain and the stakes are real. If a competent character would simply succeed, say yes and move on; if the action is impossible, say so and offer another route. Everything else is a check.

    Setting difficulty

    Usually the gamemaster does not rate difficulty at all — you roll only in challenging situations. When external factors clearly help or hinder an action, use the table. Modifiers are cumulative, and they are picked before the dice hit the table.

    Difficulty
    DifficultyModifierTypical situation
    Trivial+3No pressure, good tools, plenty of time
    Simple+2Favourable conditions, mild distraction
    Easy+1Slightly in the character's favour
    Average0Two competent people against each other
    Demanding−1Bad light, bad tools, bad weather
    Hard−2Hostile conditions, improvised equipment
    Formidable−3Everything is against it, and everyone knows

    Modifiers, classic

    A +1 modifier is one extra base die, +2 is two extra dice, and so on. Several modifiers can apply to the same roll and they are cumulative. Always add and remove dice from skills first; if you run out of skill dice, take them from gear; if you run out of gear dice, take them from attributes. With no dice left there is no chance of success — time to rethink the plan.

    Modern In the step dice variant a +1 modifier upgrades a base die one step, +2 upgrades two steps, and a −1 downgrades one step. Balance the dice as much as possible: step up the lower die first and downstep the higher one. You can never go above two D12s, and to downstep past two D6s you remove one die — you never go below one D6. If you lack the skill and roll a single base die, a step up adds a D6.

    Advantage and disadvantage, modern

    Instead of numerical modifiers, the modern variant can use advantage and disadvantage. An advantage adds a third base die, of the same type as your lower die; a disadvantage removes your lower base die, leaving one. They cancel each other one for one, and after balancing you never roll with more than a single advantage or disadvantage. Either way the roll can still be pushed.

    Help

    Another character can help if they are physically present and able to affect the outcome, declared before the dice are rolled. Each helper gives +1, to a maximum of +3 — no more than three people can help one roll. In combat, helping costs an action of the same kind (slow or fast) as the action being supported. NPCs help each other in exactly the same way.

    Opposed rolls

    To win an opposed roll you must roll more successes than your adversary; every success on one side cancels one on the other. Only the active party may push, and may decide to do so even after seeing the opponent's roll. Equal totals are a tie: the active party's action typically fails, and if the tie has to be broken, reroll the contest.

    The world answers

    There is no separate currency. For every push the gamemaster answers with a complication — a weapon misfires, something is dropped, reinforcements arrive. Its size follows the number of banes, and it is played as an ordinary event, not spent from a pool: the list of possible complications must be clear to all players, or the line between them and ordinary gamemastering blurs.

    Gamemaster characters

    NPCs use skills exactly like player characters and can push their rolls. The GM only has to roll when the NPC's action affects a PC directly; otherwise the GM simply decides what happens. For a group of identical NPCs, one initiative card for the group is enough.

    The one rule that keeps a session alive

    Fail forward. A failed roll must leave a way onward — at the cost of time, risk or resources, but a way. The door opens, but loudly; the trail is found, but the light is fading; the negotiation fails, and now someone is owed a favour.

    02

    Combat

    A fight is played on a map divided into zones — a room, a corridor, an area of ground, from a few steps across up to about 25 metres. Combat runs in rounds of roughly 5–10 seconds, and every participant acts once per round in initiative order.

    Zones and their features

    • Cluttered: moving into the zone needs a Mobility roll; on a failure you get in, but fall down.
    • Dimly lit: Observation rolls in the zone take −2, ranged attacks into it take −2, and attacks cannot pass through it.
    • Cramped: you can only crawl, never run, and you cannot move or shoot past the people next to you.

    Borders between zones are open or blocked by a wall; a blocked border has a door or hatch if the map says so, and it blocks line of sight unless you stand in the doorway and look through.

    Initiative

    Take ten cards numbered 1 to 10 — a normal deck with the face cards removed and the ace counted as 1. Every participant draws one, and the GM draws one for each NPC or NPC group. Participants act from the lowest number to the highest, starting with card #1. Initiative is drawn once, at the start of the conflict: the order stays the same for every round, so a fast character keeps their place.

    • Surprise: a character who launches a fully surprising attack chooses any initiative card they want instead of drawing; everyone else, allies included, draws normally.
    • Ambush: each victim makes an Observation roll, with −2 if the attackers are well prepared. Everyone who fails takes the bottom cards, from #10 down, drawn at random.
    • Exchanging initiative: on your turn, before acting, you may swap cards with a character whose turn has not yet come up this round. They cannot refuse and must act immediately, and they cannot swap again in the same round.
    • Hidden initiative: as a variant, keep the cards secret and redraw them at the start of every round — uncertainty instead of a fixed order.

    A turn

    A character takes one slow action and one fast action, or two fast actions. Dropping to the ground or shouting a few words are free actions: they cost nothing, but can only be taken on your own turn. Helping another character costs an action of the same kind as the action being helped.

    Movement

    A fast action moves you to a neighbouring zone, or between Short and Engaged range inside your own zone. No roll is needed unless the zone is cluttered. Crawling is a slow action, so you cannot crawl twice in a round, and in a cramped zone it is the only movement there is. A typical wooden door or hatch takes 5 points of damage before it gives in; sturdier ones have an armour rating as well.

    Retreat: with an active enemy at Engaged range and no barrier between you, moving away takes a Mobility roll. If you fail you still move, but the enemy gets an immediate free close combat attack that costs them nothing and cannot be blocked.

    Sneak attacks

    Sneaking up on someone undetected and attacking is a sneak attack. Roll Stealth, at −2 to get close enough for close combat. On a failure the enemy notices you — draw initiative. On a success the attack counts as surprising: you choose your initiative card, close combat sneak attacks get +3, and the target cannot block. Sneak attacks are always one attacker against one target.

    03

    Attacks and damage

    An attack is a skill check with a weapon. Close combat uses Melee at Engaged range, with no barrier between you and the target. Ranged attacks use Marksmanship — thrown weapons use Mobility. Success means the hit lands; additional successes mean it lands harder.

    Close combat

    Cover has no effect against close combat. A hit inflicts the weapon's base damage, and every extra success adds one point. Attacking a defenseless or unaware target gives +3, and that is not increased further if the target is also prone.

    Close combat modifiers
    FactorModifier
    Attacker prone−2
    Target prone+2
    Defenseless target+3
    Diving blow+2

    Special attacks must be declared before any dice are rolled and can only be made unarmed: wrestle an item from the opponent's grasp, trip them to the ground, push them to Short range, or pin them. A successful grapple puts both of you on the ground, makes the opponent drop their weapon and unable to move; their only option is a slow opposed Melee roll to break free, and your only option is a grapple attack — a fast unarmed attack that cannot be blocked. A diving blow is an unarmed attack in a round where you first moved into Engaged range: +2, both of you fall prone on a hit, and on a miss only you fall.

    Blocking

    When attacked in close combat you may declare a block before the attacker rolls, rolling Melee — this turns the attack into an opposed roll. Each success cancels one of the attacker's, and excess successes do nothing. Blocking is a fast action and counts against your two actions for the round. Unarmed, you can only block unarmed attacks from other humanoids; against weapons or beasts you need a sturdy weapon or tool.

    Ranged combat

    Drawing a weapon is a fast action, firing one is a slow action. The weapon's range is the furthest range category at which it can be used.

    Aiming is a fast action and gives +2 to the next shot. If you do anything but shoot afterwards, or you are hurt, the aim is lost and must be spent again. With a telescopic sight you may aim as a slow action for a further +1, for +3 in total, but you cannot slow aim and fire in the same round. An unseen target can still be shot at if you know roughly where it is, at −2.

    Automatic fire

    A burst counts as a normal ranged attack, but if it hits you may immediately attack again, the same target or another one; if that hits too, you may make a third attack, and no more. After the attacks are resolved, make a supply roll for ammunition.

    Ammo dice (optional). All firearms get a rate of fire, from 2 for a typical pistol to 5 or 6 for full-auto weapons. Add up to that many ammo dice, or up to the rounds left in the magazine minus one, whichever is lower — with one round left you cannot add any. A 6 on an ammo die is a success, but ammo dice never help you hit: if the attack hits on the base dice, each ammo success either raises the damage one step or triggers an extra hit on the same or an adjacent target in the zone. The ammunition spent is the sum of the digits on all ammo dice, plus one.

    Overwatch

    As a fast action, with a ranged weapon and no enemy at Engaged range, you can take an overwatch position facing a direction. Until your turn in the next round you may fire at a target in that direction at any point in the turn order, even before an action that has already been declared. The shot is a normal attack and uses your slow action, so you must keep that slow action free. If two characters overwatch each other and both fire, an opposed Marksmanship roll decides who shoots first; it costs no action. Overwatch is lost if you do anything else, are attacked in close combat, or suffer damage.

    Damage

    Damage equals the weapon's damage value plus one point for every success beyond the first. A hit that lands for 3 points removes 3 points of health, or imposes conditions, depending on which damage system the game uses.

    04

    Armour, cover and injuries

    Damage is not hit points in the abstract; it is the price of staying in the fight. Armour and cover reduce it, and what gets through decides whether the character stays on their feet.

    Armour

    Classic armour has a numeric rating: roll that many base dice, and every success reduces the damage by one. Modern armour has a rank from A to D: roll two base dice of that type. The roll costs no action and cannot be pushed, and armour never protects against damage you inflict on yourself by pushing.

    Degrading armour: if any damage penetrates, every bane (1) rolled on the armour dice lowers the rating by one step. If the armour absorbs all the damage, banes have no effect at all. Repairing it takes a shift of work and a successful Crafting roll, which restores one point per success up to the starting rating.

    Cover

    Taking cover in your zone is a fast action. Cover has an armour rating and works exactly like armour, but only against ranged attacks. It can be degraded like armour, and it stacks with armour: roll cover first, then armour.

    Damage systems

    Conditions

    The three physical conditions are Exhausted, Battered and Wounded; the three mental ones are Angry, Scared and Disheartened. Pushing a roll for Strength or Agility gives a physical condition of your choice, for Wits or Empathy a mental one. Each point of damage from an attack gives a physical condition; each point of potential stress gives a mental condition, reduced by one for every success on an Insight roll.

    Every physical condition gives −1 to rolls using Strength or Agility, every mental one −1 to rolls using Wits or Empathy. Take a fourth condition of the same type and you are broken, and suffer a critical injury of that type.

    Being broken and getting up

    Being broken means you are out of action: no actions, no skill rolls. You cannot go below zero in health, resolve or any attribute — further damage may give you another critical injury instead. Healing is a slow action, and only helps a broken character: each success removes one point of damage or stress. On your own you recover one point per shift, provided you are not starving, dehydrated or hypothermic; a character who is not broken also heals one point per shift.

    Critical injuries

    You suffer a physical critical injury when your health or Strength is reduced to zero, and a mental one when your resolve or Wits is reduced to zero — or when you are broken by conditions of that type. Some games also use a weapon's crit threshold, or give a critical injury on any attack that rolls two or more successes. One exception: damage from your own pushing roll never gives a critical injury, so you can never kill yourself by pushing.

    Death

    A lethal injury states a time limit — a round, a stretch or a shift. When it runs out you make a death save: a Stamina roll that costs no action. Fail and you die; succeed and you live until the next time limit, when you roll again.

    Stabilising takes Healing rolls that each consume as much time as the injury's time limit. A success pushes the limit up one category — from round to stretch, from stretch to shift — and once a shift-long injury is treated you no longer risk dying from it. A failure can be retried, but only after another death save. A character who is both broken and lethally injured needs two separate Healing rolls, in either order. Some results kill outright: no save is allowed.

    Critical injuries have a healing time in days and their effect lasts until they heal; several injures heal at the same time, and a permanent one never heals. Skill modifiers from injuries are cumulative and apply even without the skill. If someone tends you for a shift each day and makes a Healing roll, that day counts double. You can heal all your damage and still suffer the effects of a critical injury.

    05

    Hazards, vehicles and chases

    The world is a participant. Fire, cold, falling, water, poison and darkness all attack, and they do not need to roll initiative.

    Fire

    Fire has an intensity: 3 to 9 in the classic variant, D to A in the modern one, rolled as base dice. Every success is one point of damage, and if you take any damage you catch fire and burn again each round on your turn, with the intensity rising one step each round. When a fire attack inflicts no damage, the fire goes out by itself. You or a friend at Engaged range can put it out with a Mobility roll (slow action), at +2 with a blanket. Fire gives no normal critical injuries: if you are broken by it, or take fire damage while broken, you make a death save every round on your turn until you die or a Healing roll saves you.

    Explosions

    Blast power
    ClassicModernBase damage
    12A4

    Roll the blast power for everyone within Short range; the roll cannot be pushed. A success means the blast hits for its base damage, plus one for each extra success. Victims at Engaged range take one extra point. A powerful charge can reach an adjacent zone if nothing blocks it, with the blast power reduced by 6, or two steps.

    Falling, drowning and poison

    • Falling: damage equals the height in metres divided by 2, rounded down. A controlled jump allows a Mobility roll, each success reducing the damage by one.
    • Drowning: roll Stamina every stretch to stay afloat, and every round under water to hold your breath. Once you fail you take 1 point of damage to Strength each round; if you are broken while drowning, you die after a stretch. Metal armour makes you sink.
    • Poison: measured by toxicity — 3 (D) weak, 6 (C) strong, 9 (B) or more extreme. Roll Stamina against it. Lethal poison deals 1 point of damage each round until you are broken, then one more failed roll kills you; paralysing poison stops all actions for a stretch; sleeping poison lasts a shift.
    • Disease: roll Stamina against virulence (typically 6, or C). On a failure you are ill: 1 point of damage, no natural healing, and another infection roll each day, another point of damage for each failure. Broken while ill means death a day later without aid. A carer can roll your infection rolls with Healing at +2.

    Cold, hunger and sleep

    • Cold: roll Stamina at intervals set by the weather. Failure means hypothermia: 1 point of damage and 1 point of stress, no natural healing, and repeated rolls. Broken while hypothermic means death at the next roll. Only warmth ends it.
    • Starvation: one ration a day; after a day without food you stop healing damage and lose 1 point a week. Broken while starving means death a week later without food.
    • Sleep: at least one shift a day. After a day without sleep you cannot heal stress; broken by stress while sleep deprived, you collapse and sleep for a shift.

    Darkness

    Running in total darkness needs a Mobility roll, and a failure costs 1 point of damage. Attacks at Engaged range take −2; at Short range you must first succeed at Observation to find the target, also at −2; beyond that you cannot hit anything at all.

    Mounts

    When you move on a mount, roll Mobility and use the animal's Agility: each success moves you one additional zone. Only one-handed weapons can be used from the saddle, attacks against a rider take −1, and all ranged attacks from horseback take −2. Opponents choose whether to attack rider or mount. An animal whose Strength reaches zero perishes rather than taking a critical injury.

    Chases

    A chase is declared by the gamemaster, at a starting range from the distance table up to Long, measured in range categories rather than zones. Chases are played in rounds without initiative: each round every participant secretly picks a manoeuvre, the GM reveals a random obstacle, and the manoeuvres are resolved with the prey first and the pursuer last. A participant may cancel a manoeuvre but cannot pick a different one.

    06

    Magic

    Magic is not a separate game; it is a skill check with no price tag. Casting costs nothing: the only price is psychosis and the mishaps it leaves behind.

    The seven disciplines

    Druidic

    • Awareness: sight and hearing beyond the human, past and future
    • Healing: wounds, sickness, poison, even death
    • Shapeshifting: the forms, senses and instincts of animals

    Sorcerous

    • Blood Magic: life and its liquid essence
    • Death Magic: decay, the dead, the space between heartbeats
    • Elementalism: earth, wind, water and fire
    • Symbolism: signs and symbols that act when triggered

    Each discipline is a tiered specialty, and you must have it to cast its spells. Learning a new discipline or raising your rank requires a master of higher rank who is willing to teach, often at a price; without a teacher the experience cost is tripled.

    Casting a spell

    Spells are ranked 1 to 3, and a caster can use any spell at or below their rank in the discipline. Casting is usually a slow action; power words are fast, rituals take a shift or more. Roll a number of dice equal to the spell's rank; every success adds 1 to the final power.

    • Every 6 raises the power level by one: the spell is overcharged.
    • Every 1 adds psychosis (+1 at rank 1, +2 at rank 2, +3 at rank 3 and above) and causes a magic mishap on the D12 table.
    • The roll cannot be pushed, and a spell never simply fails.

    Casting one rank above your talent is possible but always causes a mishap automatically; two ranks above is impossible. Casting at a lower rank, you may drop one die per rank of difference — fewer mishaps, but also fewer overcharges. At zero dice you do not roll at all and the spell simply works.

    Grimoires. A spell cast from a book or scroll counts as one rank lower, but readying the grimoire in combat is a fast action. NPC casters have no pool: main power equals the spell's rank, and the GM rolls for overcharge and mishap as usual.

    07

    Travel and survival

    Journeys are measured on a travel map divided into hexes, one hex being ten kilometres across. A journey is measured in shifts — morning, day, evening and night — and at the start of each shift every traveller picks one task for the whole shift.

    The speed factor applies only to off-road driving; marching and driving on a road are always ×1. A lake or river needs a boat or an amphibious vehicle. Rivers can be crossed at fords and bridges, or by raft, boat or swimming.

    Tasks

    Marching, driving, gathering, keeping watch, foraging, hunting, fishing, making camp, cooking, resting and sleeping. Nobody can do more than one, and only the lookout can keep watch while marching. Passengers in a vehicle can keep watch, rest or sleep while someone else drives.

    Marching

    In good conditions you march two hexes per shift on a road or in open terrain, and one hex per shift off-road in any other terrain. In heavy rain each character makes a Stamina roll, and a failure loses one hex of movement. Marching off-road at night needs a Survival roll from one character for the whole group; a failure means no progress. Two of the four shifts a day can be marched without trouble; a third needs a Stamina roll, and a fourth, at −2, is a forced march that leaves the exhausted behind.

    Navigation: entering a new hex off-road, one character makes a Survival roll. On a failure the group enters the hex beside the intended one — right on an even roll, left on an odd one.

    Driving

    Civilian vehicles need no roll on a normal journey. At the start of each shift you drive, make a driving roll modified by terrain, and another if you enter a new terrain type; heavy rain gives −2. A failure means a mishap, usually halfway through the shift. Off-road fuel consumption is doubled, and driving off-road at night halves the speed.

    Watching, foraging and hunting

    Keeping watch: one character makes a passive Observation roll, unpushable, when the group approaches an encounter. On a success the group spots the other party first and may show itself, back off — costing an extra hex of off-road movement — or set an ambush. Travelling in a motor vehicle against people on foot gives −2 to the roll; on foot against a motorised group, +2.

    Foraging: choose food or water and roll Survival, modified by terrain. Each success is one ration. Raw plants need a sickness roll against virulence 3 (D). Only one character forages per hex per shift, and each further shift in the same hex gives a cumulative −1, reset after a week except in winter.

    Hunting: roll Survival to track, then Observation to get into position, then a ranged attack; damage equal to the animal's health or a critical hit kills it. Raw meat needs a sickness roll against virulence 6 (C). Snares catch trappable animals with another Survival roll instead of a shot.

    Fishing: in a hex beside a river, lake or sea, a character with fishing gear rolls Survival and gains one ration per success. Raw fish needs a sickness roll against virulence 6 (C). One character per hex per shift, with a cumulative −1 for each further shift there, reset after a day.

    Camp

    Making camp takes a shift and a Survival roll. On a success the group finds shelter with cover; on a failure the camp stands anyway, and the GM secretly rolls a camp mishap to spring at any time. A fire allows cooking and keeps off the cold, but its smoke gives the camp away. Sleeping without a fire in cold weather means Stamina rolls against the cold. Sleeping on bare ground skips the camp work, but everyone makes a Survival roll to find a spot, and suffers the cold.

    Cooking needs a fire or a kitchen; one shift cooks up to a dozen rations. A failed Survival roll means everyone eating must make a sickness roll — virulence 3 (D) for plants, 6 (C) for meat and fish. Resting by the fire recovers damage; sleep takes at least one shift a day. A rest or sleep interrupted by combat no longer counts. Exploring a location stops the journey for a shift or much longer, and you cannot rest or sleep if you explore for more than half a shift. Boats and rafts cross rivers and seas; sea travel works like driving, and passengers can rest or sleep.

    At the table

    Before the session

    • One place worth reaching, and one reason it is dangerous
    • Two NPCs with a want each, and one who lies
    • A clock: what happens if the players do nothing
    • The damage system in use, written where everyone can see it

    During the session

    • Say yes, or name the cost
    • Roll once, then move the fiction
    • Answer with a complication when the table gets comfortable
    • End on a decision, not on an admin task
