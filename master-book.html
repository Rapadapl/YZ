    Year Zero Engine · System reference

    Master's book

    The rules the gamemaster runs: how violence resolves, what it costs, how magic bends the world, and what the road takes from people who travel it.

    The classic variant
    Pools of d6. Damage is a number of points, armour is a number of dice, and wounds and stress are counted separately.
    The modern variant
    Ranked dice from A to D. Damage and armour talk in ranks and reliability, and the same wound can also be paid as a condition.
    01

    Running the game

    Roll when the outcome is uncertain and the stakes are real. If a competent character would simply succeed, say yes and move on; if the action is impossible, say so and offer another route. Everything else is a check.

    Setting difficulty

    Pick the modifier before the dice hit the table, and say it out loud so the table can argue with the situation rather than with the result. One modifier is usually enough; two only when the fiction clearly has two separate problems.

    Difficulty, classic variant
    DifficultyModifierTypical situation
    Elementary+3No pressure, good tools, plenty of time
    Simple+2Favourable conditions, mild distraction
    Easy+1Slightly in the character's favour
    Standard0Two competent people against each other
    Not easy−1Bad light, bad tools, bad weather

    Extreme−3Everything is against it, and everyone knows
    Modern Difficulty in the modern variant becomes advantage or penalty: an advantage adds a third die equal to the smaller of the two ranks, a penalty removes the smaller die. Both cancel before anything is rolled, so state which one applies, not how many.

    Fate points

    Fate points are the gamemaster's licence to make life interesting: an obstacle appears, a patrol turns up, a rope frays at the worst moment. Characters hand them over by pushing rolls with a special effort, which keeps the exchange honest — the more the players strain, the more the world pushes back.

    Gamemaster characters

    Give an NPC only what the scene needs: a name, one characteristic worth noticing, a skill if they are good at something, and a reason to be in the way. Full character sheets are for recurring rivals, not for the guard at the gate.

    The one rule that keeps a session alive

    Fail forward. A failed check changes the situation instead of stopping it: the door opens, but loudly; the trail is found, but the light is fading; the negotiation fails, and now someone is owed a favour.

    02

    Combat

    Combat runs in rounds. Every participant acts once per round, in initiative order, and violence is resolved the same way as any other check — it just costs something every time it happens.

    Initiative

    Initiative is dealt with cards: each participant draws a card, and the highest card acts first, counting down. Cards are drawn again at the start of every round, so a fast character is not permanently first — the situation keeps moving.

    • Surprise: a character who did not see the attack coming does not act in the first round; they can only react.
    • Exchanging initiative: two characters who want to act at the same time may swap cards by agreement, which lets a pair coordinate without a special rule.

    A turn

    A character takes one standard action and one quick action, in any order. A standard action can be traded for a second quick action, but not the other way around.

    Standard actions

    • Attack in close combat, or shoot
    • Aim, and gain a bonus to the next ranged attack
    • Use a skill that takes real effort — heal, repair, force a door
    • Full movement across the scene
    • Help another character with their action

    Quick actions

    • Move a short distance, dive for cover
    • Draw or reload a weapon
    • Shout a warning, give an order
    • Grab, drop or throw an object
    • Look around, spot a target

    Movement and distance

    Distances are descriptive: point-blank, near, far and out of sight. A character moves one zone as part of their turn, and a full standard action to cross the scene. Cover, light and noise are properties of a zone, and they matter more than exact metres.

    Keep the map in your head

    If the table has to measure something, describe the scene in zones instead: the yard, the doorway, the roof. Any two people in the same zone can reach each other; anything further needs a move or a ranged weapon.

    03

    Attacks and damage

    An attack is a skill check with a weapon. Close combat uses Near combat and Body; ranged attacks use Ranged and Dexterity. Success means the hit lands; additional successes mean it lands harder.

    Aiming and automatic fire

    Aiming spends a standard action and grants a bonus to the next ranged attack in the same or the following round. Automatic fire spends a standard action and empties a burst: the attack gains a bonus, and the shooter may spread successes across several targets in the same zone.

    Damage

    Damage equals the weapon's damage value plus one point for every success beyond the first. A hit that lands for 3 points removes 3 points of health, or imposes conditions, depending on which damage system the game uses.

    Armour

    Classic Armour has a numeric value. Roll that many dice; each success reduces incoming damage by 1. A curse on the armour roll — a 1 — reduces the armour's value by 1 until repaired with Craft.
    Modern Armour has a rank from A to D. Roll its two dice; each success reduces damage by 1, and each point of damage that gets through lowers the armour's reliability by one step. At zero reliability the item is destroyed.
    Armour never makes a fight safe

    Armour buys a round, not immunity. Because armour degrades — by curses in the classic variant, by reliability in the modern one — a long fight strips everyone down to their skin.

    04

    Injuries and conditions

    Damage is not hit points in the abstract; it is the price of staying in the fight. Three systems exist for paying it, and a game picks one before the first session.

    The three damage systems
    SystemHow damage is trackedTaken out when
    Health and resolveTwo pools, one physical and one mentalEither pool reaches zero
    Characteristic reductionThe characteristic used by the attack is reducedAny characteristic reaches zero
    ConditionsThree physical and three mental slotsA fourth condition of the same type is required

    Conditions

    Physical conditions — exhausted, battered, injured — impose −1 on every Body and Dexterity check. Mental conditions — annoyed, frightened, depressed — impose −1 on every Wits and Empathy check. With three of one type already marked, the next one takes the character out.

    Being taken out

    A character who is taken out is out of the scene and takes an injury. They are not necessarily dead: death is a separate, rarer result, and it always comes with a chance to resist.

    Death

    Lethal injuries kill after a stated interval — a round, a period, a shift. When the interval runs out, the character makes an Endurance check. Success means survival until the next interval and another check; failure means death. Some results kill outright, and no check helps. Another character may stabilise the wounded with Healing and the right equipment before the interval runs out.

    Healing

    Another character can spend a standard action on a Healing check; every success removes one wound or one stress point, and only a taken-out character can be helped this way. Without help, a character recovers one point per shift — but only if they are not hungry, thirsty or freezing. Injuries themselves heal over a shift with a successful Craft check, which restores one point of quality or reliability; a failure fixes the damage in place for good.

    05

    Hazards

    The world is a participant. Fire, cold, falling, water, poison and darkness all attack, and they do not need to roll initiative.

    Situational damage
    HazardWhat happens
    FireAttacks with an intensity rating. Each round in the flames deals that much damage; armour counts, but burning clothes keep the fire on the character until put out.
    ColdAn Endurance check per period in freezing conditions. Failure deals damage or a condition, and wet clothing makes the check harder.
    FallingDamage rises with the height. A short fall hurts; a fall from a roof ends a character. A Dexterity check may soften the landing.
    DrowningEndurance check to hold breath, then damage each round once the air is gone. Cold water shortens the clock.
    PoisonHas a toxicity rating rolled as dice; every success deals damage. Paralytic and sleeping poisons also impose conditions rather than mere wounds.
    DarknessEverything depending on sight takes a penalty; ranged attacks into the dark are guesses, and Observation checks fail before they start.
    Heat and thirstAn Endurance check per shift in punishing heat. Failure costs a point of health, and water becomes the most valuable item in the pack.
    Hazards should be telegraphed

    Describe the slick floor, the smoke under the door, the ice on the handrail. A hazard nobody could see coming is a tax; a hazard the players chose to risk is a decision.

    06

    Chases, vehicles and cover

    A chase is a contest of position, run as a series of opposed checks. The quarry starts with a lead measured in successes; the pursuer erases it one success at a time, and the chase ends when the lead is gone or the quarry breaks line of sight.

    Manoeuvres

    Each round, both sides describe a manoeuvre and roll the skill it implies: a sprint is Dexterity, a shortcut is Survival or Observation, a ram is Drive, a bluff is Persuasion. The winner of the opposed check adjusts the lead and describes what changed — a gate slammed, a corner taken too fast, a shortcut through a flooded basement.

    Vehicles

    Vehicles have a speed on roads and a speed off them, a structure value that absorbs damage, and a fuel supply. In a collision the vehicle takes damage equal to the speed it was doing, and everyone inside takes a share of that. A vehicle reduced to zero structure is wrecked, and the wreck is cover.

    Cover

    Cover adds dice to defence or subtracts them from the attacker, depending on how much of the target it hides. Light cover — a fence, a curtain — is a small modifier; hard cover — a wall, an engine block — is a large one. Cover and armour stack, but cover can be destroyed, and the character behind it can be flanked.

    07

    Magic

    Magic is not a separate game; it is a skill check with a different currency. Spells are paid for in fate points, ranked from 1 to 3, and the cost of reaching beyond what a character has learned is paid in side effects.

    The seven traditions

    Druidic

    • Nature: growth, weather, beasts and the slow patience of the wild
    • Elemental: water, fire, air and stone, called and shaped
    • Symbolism: signs cut, painted or worn, which act when triggered

    Sorcerous

    • Blood: strength, heat and binding, paid in the caster's own body
    • Death: decay, fear, the dead and the space between heartbeats
    • Elementalism: the raw elements as weapons rather than as allies
    • Mind: perception, memory and the wills of other people

    Learning magic

    Magic is learned as a specialization with ranks. A character with a magical specialization can cast every spell of that rank and below. Learning from a teacher costs the normal price; learning alone costs triple, and some things cannot be learned alone at all. A teacher must be higher rank than the student, and must be convinced — often paid.

    Casting a spell

    1. Choose the spell and the target.
    2. Spend at least one fate point. The number spent is the spell's main power.
    3. Roll that many d6. Every 6 adds 1 to the final power; any 1 triggers a side effect.
    4. Resolve the effect at its final power: damage, duration, distance, or the number of targets.

    A spell can never fail outright, and a special effort cannot be applied to casting. Casting one rank above the character's specialization is allowed, but always causes a side effect. Two ranks above is impossible.

    Safe casting When casting a spell below the character's rank, they may roll one die fewer for each rank of difference. Fewer dice means fewer side effects — and fewer chances to enhance the spell. Reduce to zero dice and no roll is needed: the spell simply works.

    Common spells

    • Sense magic — feel a spell cast nearby, or read the charge on an object.
    • Magical printing — protect a person or place from magic for a shift.
    • Dispel magic — interfere with another caster's working.
    • Hide magic — conceal a spell or an enchanted object from sight.
    • Grimoire — a written spell counts as one rank lower, but takes a quick action to open.
    • Enchanted items — bind a spell to an object; the extra fate points decide how long the binding lasts.
    • Fate transfer — give fate points to another caster, or take them from the world around.
    • Shapechange — wear another shape for as long as the power holds.
    • Blood magic — power bought with the caster's own harm.
    • Death magic — speak with the dead, wither the living, and pay for both.
    Magic has witnesses

    Every spell leaves a trace someone can read, and every side effect is a scene. When a character casts, ask what it looks like from outside the circle — that is what the world remembers.

    08

    Travel and survival

    The map is divided into sectors, and the journey is measured in shifts: morning, afternoon, evening and night. A day of travel is two shifts of walking; pushing to three or four costs Endurance checks that fail in ways the whole group has to argue about.

    Movement

    Terrain and its modifiers
    TerrainSpeedForagingHuntingDriving
    Open×1−1+1+1
    Forest×1+10−1
    Hills×½0−10
    Mountains×½−20−1
    Desert×¼−2−10
    Swamp×½−10−2

    Two sectors per shift on a road in good conditions; one sector per shift anywhere else. Heavy rain calls for an Endurance check, and a failure costs a sector. Moving from a roadless sector into the next needs a Survival check to navigate: a failure lands the group in an adjacent sector, decided by a die roll.

    Encounters

    Each shift carries a chance of an encounter, at a distance set by the terrain: near in ruins, far almost everywhere else, and at the very edge of sight in forest. The group's scout makes a passive Insight check; success means the group sees first and can hide, retreat or set an ambush. A group on foot has an easier time spotting a vehicle than the other way around.

    Weather, rest and water

    • Weather changes the modifiers on everything: rain ruins tracks and powder, heat empties canteens, cold kills the careless at night.
    • Rest is a shift without travel; sleep is a shift without a watch. A character who misses both starts taking penalties that no amount of coffee fixes.
    • Water travel uses boats and barges: faster on rivers, but the encounters are at close range and the shore is the only escape.
    Travel is a resource game

    Every journey costs time, fuel, food and nerves. Decide which of the four the players are short of before the session, and let the road charge them for it. The journey is not the gap between fights; it is where the fight begins.

    09

    At the table

    Before the session

    • One place worth reaching, and one reason it is dangerous
    • Two NPCs with a want each, and one who lies
    • A clock: what happens if the players do nothing
    • The damage system in use, written where everyone can see it

    During the session

    • Say yes, or name the cost
    • Roll once, then move the fiction
    • Spend fate points when the table gets comfortable
    • End on a decision, not on an admin task
